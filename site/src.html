Computer Vision course project · Adani University · YOLO11 on broadcast football

Ballhawk

On a wide broadcast shot the football is about 3.7 px across by the time YOLO sees it. The finest standard detection grid works in 8 px cells, so the ball is smaller than one cell.

This project measures how badly that hurts, tests five ways to fix it one variable at a time, and follows the result into tracking, team assignment and an offside visualiser.

Phase 1 · Detection study

What moves ball detection

Every run is YOLO11s for 100 epochs with seed 0, and changes one thing against the baseline. Settings are chosen on the validation split and reported once on test. The baseline and the two smallest effects were repeated with three seeds to measure the noise any gain has to beat.

Resolution

Works

Training at 960 and 1280 lifts ball mAP50-95 from 0.110 to 0.242 and 0.298, with validation agreeing (0.117 → 0.321 → 0.390). At 1280 the typical ball grows to about 7.4 px, close to one grid cell. The T4 ran out of memory at batch 16, so the 1280 run used batch 8, recorded as a second variable.

Sliced inference

Overlaps

Tiling each frame into 960 px slices lifts the 640 model from 0.113 to 0.188 at about a ninth of the speed. On the 1280 model it hurts on validation (0.381 → 0.366). Both fix the same shortage of pixels on the ball, so the gains do not stack.

Scale jitter, class weights

Not established

With one seed, narrowing scale jitter from 0.5 to 0.2 and up-weighting rare classes each looked like +0.02 on test. Repeated with three seeds on one platform, the gains shrink to about +0.007 on test and +0.008 and 0.000 on validation, against a seed-to-seed spread of 0.009 to 0.014. Neither is established at this data size. Adding motion blur on top of scale 0.2 gives nothing for the ball and lowers all-class mAP from 0.502 to 0.475.

P2 head

Explained

A stride-4 head scored worse (0.045). No YOLO11 P2 config ships with the library, so the head was built from the stock config and only 297 of 593 weight tensors carried over from pretraining. A control that re-initialises the same layers of the stock model scores 0.060. The loss comes from discarded pretraining; the finer head itself changes nothing measurable.

Where the ball goes missing

These are test-split balls the baseline misses at confidence 0.25, cropped at native resolution. Some are hidden by legs or crowded feet, some are blurred or sit on a painted line. Several are isolated balls in open grass, clearly visible at 1920 px and lost only when the frame is shrunk to 640.

Phase 2 · Tracking and teams

A better detector keeps the ball in view

ByteTrack runs on a 17-second clip, and players are split into teams by clustering their shirt colour, with one vote per track across frames.

On the phone clip, tracks fragment heavily: 261 to 448 player IDs in 17 seconds, and camera-motion compensation (BoT-SORT) removes only 5 to 14 % of them. On broadcast footage the same pipeline creates 101 to 130 IDs in 30 seconds. Shirt colour is measured against each frame's own grass colour, after a fixed "green" mask turned out to erase lime-green kits.

Phases 3 to 5 · Pitch geometry

Offside is a calibration problem

A homography maps each frame onto a 105 × 68 m pitch model, anchored on penalty-area geometry that the laws fix. Under Law 11 a player is offside when in the opponents' half and nearer the goal line than both the ball and the second-last defender, so the line sits at whichever of those two is nearer the goal line. This is an offside visualiser, not VAR: feet are the bottom of a bounding box, and only visible players count.

By hand

Fitting on the four penalty-box corners that annotators clicked by hand leaves a median error of 3.7 px on the other labelled points. Each pixel of click error moves the offside line about 10 cm at the far touchline, so careful manual calibration alone carries roughly 35 to 40 cm of uncertainty. Offside is decided in centimetres, which is why real VAR uses calibrated multi-camera rigs.

Keypoint model

A YOLO11s pose model finds 32 pitch landmarks and fits a homography with RANSAC, calibrating every test frame. Four landmarks were defined wrongly in the reference layout the dataset is usually paired with: annotators placed them where the penalty arc meets the box line, 1.85 m from where the layout says. Fixing that cut their residual from about 2 m to 0.2 m. At the same batch size, training at 1280 instead of 960 cuts the validation error from 4.0 m to 2.9 m, so resolution helps here too. Batch size matters more: the 960 model trained with batches of 16 reaches 2.7 m and stays in use, because the T4 fits only 4 images per batch at 1280.

On video

On an unseen 30-second broadcast clip, every one of 750 frames calibrates and the offside line moves a median 8.5 cm from one frame to the next; 12 % of steps exceed 1 m. On a phone clip filmed low in the stands the median step is 2.1 m and 62 % of steps exceed 1 m, with visibly wrong geometry. The keypoint model works on the camera it was trained on and does not transfer to other angles.

Limits

What this does not show

  • Training has 226 images. Three-seed repeats put seed-to-seed noise at about 0.01 mAP, so only the resolution effect is clearly real; smaller effects would need more data or many more seeds.
  • The 1280 run used batch 8, so its gain mixes resolution with a smaller batch.
  • Track fragmentation is much lower on broadcast footage than on the phone clip, but it is measured without identity labels, so true ID switches are not counted.
  • Working out which team is defending from single frames is unreliable, so offside calls on stills are not reported as decisions.
  • Foot position is the bottom of a box. Real offside is judged on the foremost body part with calibrated multi-camera rigs.
